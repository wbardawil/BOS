# Worked exemplars: three cascades

These three cascades — drawn from HBR's *Playing to Win* training materials — anchor the coach's reasoning. The first is "plausible but soft," the second is the textbook "Playing to Play" failure, and the third is the reframed "Playing to Win" version of the same business.

The coach uses these as comparison points, **not** as templates to copy.

---

## Exemplar 1 — JJS Cleaning Co. (a plausible-but-soft cascade)

A mid-market services company. Coherent on paper, but the trade-offs aren't sharp enough yet.

- **Winning Aspiration.** Be the leading office-cleaning company in Chicago; carry the highest client loyalty and repeat-business rate in the industry.
- **Where to Play.** Service contracts (daily / weekly) plus à-la-carte specialty deep-cleans, sold directly to small and medium enterprises in the Chicago area.
- **How to Win.** Differentiation through service excellence — deep relationships with decision makers, customized offerings, friendly knowledgeable staff every time.
- **Capabilities.** Recruiting and retention of adaptable service-oriented teams; sales and customer service; quality control and accountability.
- **Management Systems.** Logistics and fleet; compensation and rewards; CRM.

**What the coach should flag**

- The HTW promise ("service excellence" + "deep relationships") is the kind of thing every services competitor claims. Push for the *structural* reason it's defensible here.
- The WTP doesn't say what JJS will *not* do — large enterprises? other metros? residential? — so capital can leak.
- The Capabilities are real but generic. Which one is the *hardest for a competitor to copy*?

## Exemplar 2 — Oil of Olay BEFORE (the "Playing to Play" trap)

The textbook example of a cascade that sounds strategic but is actually participation.

- **Winning Aspiration.** Play in the global skin-care category; turn a shrinking ~$750M business into a growing one.
- **Where to Play.** Women aged 50+, sold through discount, grocery, and drugstore channels, low-price products, with brands and SKUs that vary by country.
- **How to Win.** Leverage scale across a broad array of widely available products; "classic" brand positioning.
- **Capabilities.** Line-extension R&D; strong retail relationships.
- **Management Systems.** Market share, in-store presence, in-stock positions; product R&D.

**What the coach should flag**

- The aspiration is **financial-only** ("turn shrinking into growing") and names no rival to beat — a classic vacuum.
- WTP is everywhere/everyone — the segment is broad, the channel is broad, the product is broad, and the geography is fragmented. There is no field on which the company is decisively strong.
- HTW is "scale" — which is the same advantage the much bigger competitors already have. Not a real source of advantage.
- Capabilities and management systems sustain the *current* business; nothing here would build a new one.

## Exemplar 3 — Oil of Olay AFTER (a "Playing to Win" reframe)

The reframe that became the case study for the "masstige" category.

- **Winning Aspiration.** Win convincingly in skin-care; build a pillar for the broader beauty business.
- **Where to Play.** Younger women just noticing the first signs of aging; mass channels (where the best customers are); new products / formulations that fight multiple aging signs; a consistent global brand.
- **How to Win.** Move upmarket in product, price, and promotion; establish a "masstige" presence — department-store-grade product at mass-channel reach.
- **Capabilities.** Innovation across product / distribution / packaging; deep consumer understanding; superior go-to-market; partnering.
- **Management Systems.** Partnering systems; channel and consumer metrics.

**What the coach should flag (as a positive model)**

- The pivot wasn't a new product — it was changing **who** (younger women), **where** (mass channels at premium prices), and **how to win** (a named structural move: "masstige").
- All four WTP dimensions — customer / channel / product / geography — are answered explicitly.
- HTW names a structural move that no incumbent in either tier (mass or department-store) could easily replicate.
- Capabilities clearly *reinforce* HTW: consumer understanding feeds the targeting, packaging innovation supports the premium price, partnering enables mass-channel placement at department-store quality.

## How the coach uses these

When a user submits a cascade, compare against:

- **Exemplar 2 failure modes.** Is the aspiration financial-only? Is WTP everywhere/everyone? Is HTW "scale" or another generic claim?
- **Exemplar 3 strengths.** Are all four WTP dimensions answered? Does HTW name a *structural* move? Do capabilities reinforce HTW?
- **Exemplar 1 question.** "This is plausible — what makes it defensible against a competent competitor who copies the visible parts?"

For users at the **$10M–$150M revenue band**, calibrate:

- Tests that fit a 4–12 week cycle and a 50–500 person org (skip $1M brand-tracker studies).
- Aspirations that name a *winnable* field (a region, segment, or job-to-be-done), not a global category.
- Capabilities expressed as 3–5 reinforcing activities the existing team can actually develop, not 10-year platform bets.
